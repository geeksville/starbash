## post process

First off, unless you've reverse-engineered Russell Croman's code, BlurXTerminator doesn't run natively inside Siril. I'm assuming you're exporting your linear data to Photoshop or Affinity Photo to use the RC Astro plugins.

**How to combine them in Siril:**

1. Go to the **Image Processing** menu and open **RGB Compositing**.
2. Assign the files to the classic Hubble palette:
* **Red** = SII
* **Green** = Ha
* **Blue** = OIII


3. Hit align, Linear Match (don't adjust layers brightness), adjust your mix if necessary, and save the combined file.

**When to Deblur/Denoise:**
Do it **BEFORE** you combine them, while the files are still individual, linear mono masters.

Why? Because physics. Your Ha channel is likely sharp and glorious, while your SII channel is probably a noisy, bloated mess. Running BlurXTerminator on the individual mono channels allows the AI to calculate the exact Point Spread Function (PSF) and noise profile for each specific wavelength.

If you combine them first, you're just mathematically smearing your crisp Ha data with your sloppy SII/OIII data before trying to fix it. Fix the ingredients before you bake the cake.

### prevent green

Let’s kill the green monster. Since your Ha signal is astronomically (pun intended) stronger than your Sii and Oiii, standard stretching turns the Hubble palette into a toxic green blob.

Here is the exact order of operations to stop that from happening:

1. **Background Extraction:** Do this *before* you stretch, while the image is still linear. Narrowband gradients will ruin the whole process if you bake them in.
2. **Generalized Hyperbolic Stretch (GHS):** Stop using the basic histogram transformation like a caveman. GHS lets you gently, iteratively stretch the shadows and midtones without blowing out your highlights or warping the color balance as aggressively.
3. **Remove Green Noise (SCNR):** This is the magic button. Once you're stretched, your image will still probably look intensely green. Go to **Image Processing > Remove Green Noise** and let it rip. This specifically attacks the Ha dominance and shifts the palette into those glorious Hubble golds and deep blues.
4. **Color Saturation:** Now that the green is dead, open the Color Saturation tool and gently bump the global saturation, or selectively target the yellows and blues to taste.

**Crucial Warning:** Do *not* try to run Photometric Color Calibration (PCC) on SHO data. The catalog expects natural, broadband stars, not your narrowband science project. It will just spit out an error and judge you.

### workflow

Run StarNet right after your Background Extraction, while the data is still linear.

If you stretch the nebula and the stars at the same time, you'll inevitably bloat your stars into massive, featureless white golf balls while trying to pull out the faint dust. Split them up so you can abuse the nebula data without ruining the stars.

Here is the exact workflow:

1. **Prep the canvas:** Combine into SHO, Crop out the stacking artifacts, and run Background Extraction.
2. **Nuke the stars:** Go to **Image Processing > Star Processing**. Check the "Pre-stretch linear image" box. This temporarily stretches the data in memory so the StarNet neural net can actually find the stars, but it spits out pristine linear files.
3. **Stretch the Nebula:** Go to town with Generalized Hyperbolic Stretch (GHS) on the starless image. Push the faint signal as hard as you want.
4. **Stretch the Stars:** Open your star mask and stretch it gently—using Asinh Transformation or basic Histogram Transformation. Keep them tight and small.
5. **Recombine:** Use **Image Processing > Star Recomposition** to blend the tight stars back onto your stretched nebula.

### fix purple stars

Ah, magenta stars. The universal badge of dishonor for SHO imagers.

You get these because when you nuked the green channel earlier to fix the nebula, you stripped the green (Ha) out of the stars, leaving only Red (Sii) and Blue (Oiii). Red + Blue = a cosmic Prince concert.

Since you wisely separated your stars in the last step, fixing this takes about ten seconds using the classic "Invert Trick" on your star file:

1. **Invert the image:** Go to **Image Processing > Negative**. Your magenta stars are now violently green.
2. **Kill the green:** Go to **Image Processing > Remove Green Noise** (SCNR) and apply it. This completely eliminates that newly formed green signal.
3. **Invert it back:** Go to **Image Processing > Negative** one more time.

Boom. The magenta is gone, leaving you with nice, neutral, white-ish stars to composite back over your nebula.

> **Pro-Tip:** If you want truly gorgeous star colors, dump the SHO stars entirely. Shoot 30 minutes of standard RGB frames, run StarNet on *that*, and composite those natural-colored stars onto your narrowband nebula.


### sequence

First off, "Veralux hypermetric stretch" sounds like a warp drive component from Star Trek. You're thinking of **Generalized Hyperbolic Stretch (GHS)**.

You run GHS **after** you separate your stars with StarNet, but **before** you remove the green noise (SCNR).

Here is your master timeline so you don't get lost in the weeds:

0. cuav says do background extraction first (on individual linear mono files)
1. **Deblur then denoise** (on individual linear mono files)
3. **Combine** into SHO
4. **StarNet** (split stars from the nebula while everything is still linear - NOTE: starnet does better than the starexterminator plugin)
5. **GHS (This is where you stretch your starless nebula)**
6. **SCNR** (Nuke the green)
7. **Color Saturation**
8. **Star Recomposition** (Blend your separately stretched stars back in)

Stretch the starless image, not the combined one, unless you want your stars looking like blown-out headlights.
